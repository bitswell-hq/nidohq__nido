\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Proofs about DKIM-signed email.} The zkEmail
project~\cite{zkemail-docs,zkemail-verify,zkemail-nr} proves statements about
DKIM-signed email in zero knowledge, with Circom circuits and a Noir library,
and its libraries have been audited
publicly~\cite{audit-zksecurity-2024,audit-yacademy,audit-zellic-2024,audit-matterlabs-2024,audit-veridise-2024,audit-consensys-2024}.
An email wallet built on it lets users control a contract wallet by sending
emails, verified by a proof of the provider's signature~\cite{suegami-email-wallet}.
Its account-recovery modules~\cite{zkemail-recovery} make email guardians of
ERC-4337~\cite{erc4337} and ERC-7579~\cite{erc7579} smart accounts: each
guardian is a contract whose address derives from a hash of the email
address and an account code, a guardian approves by replying to a request
email whose command names the account, and a weighted threshold and a delay
apply. In the underlying SDK~\cite{zkemail-tx-builder} a relayer receives
the user's email, generates the proof, and stores users' account codes. The
design here differs in where trust sits: the proof is made in the user's
browser, no operator holds the account code or the email, the email binds a
recovery statement blinded from the provider rather than a readable command,
and the key registry has no immediate path for a new key. It is also one
factor inside an existing recovery protocol, with properties proven over that
protocol's controller.

\paragraph{Proofs about OIDC credentials.} zkLogin~\cite{zklogin}
authenticates blockchain transactions with OpenID Connect tokens from
existing platforms and hides the link between the off-chain and on-chain
identity; Aptos's keyless accounts~\cite{aptos-aip61} do the same and name a
proof of a DKIM-signed email as a possible recovery path for accounts whose
managing application disappears. In both, the credential is the account's key
rather than one factor among several. An OIDC path here would be a second way
to produce the email factor, never a factor independent of the same
provider's DKIM, since one provider controls both.

\paragraph{Guardian and server-based recovery.} Social recovery
wallets~\cite{buterin-social-recovery} let a majority of guardians replace
the signing key; in Argent's wallet~\cite{argent-spec} guardians, possibly a
paid service, trigger a recovery the owner can cancel during a security
period; Stellar's SEP-30~\cite{sep30} has recovery servers that hold signing
keys and authenticate the user by email or phone. These place the email
check, and the address, with a guardian or a server; the design here keeps
their delay and veto and removes the operator from the check.

\paragraph{Credentials from existing infrastructure.} DECO~\cite{deco}
proves statements about TLS sessions, and zk-creds~\cite{zkcreds} builds
anonymous credentials from existing identity documents; like email proofs,
both turn an authority's existing signature into a credential and inherit
that authority's trust.

\paragraph{DKIM attributability.} KeyForge~\cite{specter-keyforge} proposes
that providers publish expired signing keys so stolen mail cannot be proven
authentic. Email proofs rely on exactly the attributability KeyForge
removes: a provider that publishes old private keys turns every registry or
pin entry for them into a forgeable key. The registry's retirement, grace
and revocation rules, and the pin's bounded lifetime, are the levers such a
policy would act on.

\paragraph{Account recovery and takeover.} Email is a common recovery
channel at large services~\cite{bonneau-recovery}, and manual hijackers who
take over an inbox use it to lock out the owner~\cite{bursztein-hijacking}:
the inbox thief of \S\ref{sec:threat}, and the reason email here is never
sufficient alone and alerts reach a second channel.
