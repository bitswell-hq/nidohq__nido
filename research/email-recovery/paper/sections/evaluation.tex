\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

The evaluation asks three questions: does every attack the threat model
names fail at the layer the design assigns it to (\S\ref{sec:eval-attacks});
what does an email vote cost on chain, in each environment it runs in
(\S\ref{sec:eval-cost}); and how long does a user wait for a proof
(\S\ref{sec:eval-proving}). It ends with the alert path, the end-to-end runs
on a local network and on testnet, and the threats to validity.

\paragraph{Environments.} The \emph{test host} is Soroban's in-process host
in the integration tests, with Perch's controller and account from Perch's
deployed WebAssembly and every email-recovery contract from its own; the
ledger can be advanced, so it reaches timelocks that a network cannot within
a run. The \emph{local network} is \texttt{stellar/quickstart} in local mode,
pinned by digest (protocol 28, Stellar RPC 29.0.1, one ledger about every
second), onto which the suite deploys Perch's stack and the email-recovery
contracts from WebAssembly. \emph{Testnet} runs protocol 29 behind Stellar RPC
30.0.0, with Perch's deployment and the email-recovery contracts deployed from
this repository. Every approval in every environment carries a real proof of
a recovery email signed by a provider mock for the statement the real
controller built. Proving runs on one desktop (Apple M5 Max, 18 logical CPUs:
6 performance and 12 efficiency cores, 128 GiB, macOS 26.5.2) shared with
other work, whose load average is recorded with each
run\claim{environments}.

\subsection{Attack suite}\label{sec:eval-attacks}
The suite deploys a registry with the proposed governance, whose launch keys
wait in their 72-hour timelock throughout the run, and enrolls each account as
the wallet does, with a 30-day pin and a recovery member built by the
wallet's own builder. Each scenario gets the attacker's strongest input: the
signature a standard DKIM verifier accepts, the octets it covers, the claimed
account's key tree or one planted with the attacker's key, and that account's
real commitment and statement. Where a forged email still yields a witness (a
key no accepted tree holds, a guessed account code), the suite submits a real
proof, so the refusal is the chain's, decoded to its layer: a circuit
assertion, a guardian check, a registry or Perch error, the code backup, or
the alert path. Three registry scenarios need an elapsed timelock or grace
period, which a local network cannot reach within a run; they run on the test
host with the registry's production WebAssembly and real timelocks.

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:attack-summary} summarizes the 56 rows,
53 on the local network\claim{network-attack-suite} and 3 on the test
host\claim{registry-timelock-attacks}; every observed outcome is the expected
one. Forged emails fail at the circuit, a real proof under a key no tree
holds at check 11, and a 1024-bit key or a domain outside the allowlist at
the registry. One forged row is accepted by design: a body replaced after
signing counts one vote, because the provider signed the header block, which
is all the proof binds. Replays fail at whichever of checks 1, 2, 11 and 12
the replayed approval fails first, or at Perch (\texttt{AlreadyCounted});
another account's pin root fails check 11 because pins are salted. A planted
key is refused during its timelock, can be stopped by two members, and after
activation cannot approve without the account code; a revoked key's root is
refused in the revocation's ledger, a superseded root one ledger after its
grace. Encoded-word and split Subjects are refused, while folded Subjects and
every launch domain's format count one vote each.

\paragraph{The compromised inbox.} An attacker who holds the inbox but not the
account code fails at the circuit (the \texttt{From} address does not open the
commitment) and, with a real proof for its own guess, at check 11; the backup
in the Sent folder does not open without its passphrase. With the code as
well, the email vote is one vote: under a 2-of-2 quorum the attempt stays
collecting. With the email guardian as the only guardian, under
\texttt{Loss} and a seven-day delay, the attacker authorizes an attempt but
cannot complete it within the delay; the owner receives the attempt's code by
email and by Web Push and cancels it, and a new attempt needs a new
email\claim{compromised-inbox-alerted}. A copied approval submitted first by
an observer records the guardian's single vote for the same attempt and
cannot be redirected to a cancellation, another attempt or another
account\claim{front-running-one-vote}. No envelope or result of the run's 165
transactions contains any of its 17 inboxes' addresses or account
codes\claim{privacy-no-address-on-chain}.

\subsection{On-chain cost}\label{sec:eval-cost}
Table~\ref{tab:cost} gives one \texttt{submit\_guardian} carrying an email
approval with a real proof, read three ways. On the test host the reading is
the invocation's cost estimate, and the same call re-executed as a signed
transaction with the authorization entry enforced, priced at the mainnet fee
rates the Soroban SDK records\claim{onchain-verification-cost}. On the local
network and testnet it is the host's own metering of each transaction and the
fee the network charged\claim{network-verification-cost}\claim{testnet-email-recovery}.
The cost does not depend on the provider: the proof, the public inputs and the
circuit's size are the same for every email. Every reading enforces
the guardian's authorization entry; a simulation that only records
authorization does not run a custom account's \texttt{\_\_check\_auth} and
would leave out the guardian's checks and the proof verification.

\paragraph{Why the readings differ.} Only the first of three differences is
attributed. \emph{Key path}: network approvals use the pin, so the guardian
never calls the registry, whereas the test host's use a registry root,
putting the registry's instance and code in the footprint and costing about
564{,}000 instructions for \texttt{is\_accepted\_root}\claim{registry-costs}.
\emph{Metering}: each host meters with its own cost parameters, which are not
compared here; the local count is below the test host's by more than the
registry call, testnet's (a newer protocol) about 1.6 million above the local
one, and the test host's transaction reading about 7.6\% above its invocation
reading, which the SDK documents omits some production work. \emph{Fees}:
each environment prices with its own settings; testnet charged about half of
what the local network did. The vote that authorizes an attempt also writes the authorized
attempt and pays its rent: 3{,}734{,}680 stroops on the local network, of
which 3{,}550{,}194 are rent\claim{authorizing-vote-cost}. Bytes written and
the 16{,}784-byte authorization entry around the 16{,}224-byte
proof\claim{onchain-verification-cost} are the same everywhere.

\paragraph{Phones are not measured.} No phone was available. The design
budgets up to about two minutes of proving on a phone, behind a progress
indicator; that budget is a target, not a result\claim{phone-proving-budget}.
Two desktop proxies bound what can be said. The desktop's efficiency cores,
selected with macOS's background scheduling class, prove in 36.4 seconds on
one thread in Chrome, 56.6 in Node and 18.2 natively\claim{efficiency-core-proving};
they are a slower class of core, not a model of a phone. DevTools CPU
throttling at 4x and 6x leaves proving unchanged, because Chrome throttles
pages but refuses to throttle the workers in which all proving
runs\claim{devtools-throttling}. A self-contained benchmark page that runs the
same prover on any device, with no account, is part of the wallet.

\subsection{Alerts, end to end, and testnet}\label{sec:eval-e2e}
\paragraph{Alerts.} With every service a loopback mock, an
\texttt{attempt\_authorized} event reaches the sending mock in 22 to 28\,ms
(median 26) and the push mock in 29 to 35\,ms (median 33), and a restarted
vault restores its key from two custodians in 244 to 339\,ms (median 302),
over ten runs each\claim{alert-latency}. These figures show the service's own
cost; a deployment adds the watcher's polling interval and the providers'
delivery.

\paragraph{End to end.} On a local network, a browser test drives the wallet
through enrollment (the Gmail mock signs the enrollment email, the vault seals
it back, the page checks it, registers the alert contact with Web Push,
deploys the guardian with its pin, and lists it with a quorum of two) and,
after the passkey is lost, through recovery: the page proves the recovery
email in the browser, submits the vote, a second helper approves, the alert
email and push carry the code the page shows, and after the delay the page
completes the recovery\claim{end-to-end-local}.

\paragraph{Testnet.} On testnet, through Perch's deployment and Nido's
factory, one complete email-factor recovery ran: mint, guardian deployment,
the recovery document (quorum two with one helper, a 12-ledger delay),
\texttt{begin\_lost\_key}, the email vote with a real proof, the helper's
vote, the delay, and completion, after which the account's document held the
new passkey and not the lost one\claim{testnet-email-recovery}. The seven
transactions were charged 13{,}384{,}714 stroops, most of it rent for new
entries; the run first restored Perch's doc compiler and recovery controller,
which had been archived on testnet\claim{testnet-recovery-total}.

\subsection{Threats to validity}\label{sec:eval-threats}
Every email is signed by a mock in its provider's published layout, and DNS,
the sending services, the push service, the enclave and the custodians are
mocks: a provider whose real mail differs from its mock is not covered. No
registry addition leaves its timelock during a network run, so network
approvals use the pin, and scenarios that need an active key or an elapsed
grace period run on the test host, which runs the same contracts but is not a
network; likewise the enclave-image registry's 14-day timelock, for which the
local alert path reads a test-only allowlist. A scenario reports only the
first layer that refuses it; later checks are covered by unit tests. Timing
comes from one shared machine, and the testnet figures from one run.
