\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

The evaluation asks whether every attack the threat model names fails at the
layer the design assigns it to (\S\ref{sec:eval-attacks}), whether the
circuit reads real and fuzzed headers as reference implementations do
(\S\ref{sec:eval-parser}), what an email vote costs in each environment and
on mainnet (\S\ref{sec:eval-cost}), and how long a user waits for a proof
(\S\ref{sec:eval-proving}).

\paragraph{Environments.} The \emph{test host} is Soroban's in-process host
in the integration tests, with Perch's controller and account from Perch's
deployed WebAssembly and every email-recovery contract from its own; its
clock can be advanced, so it reaches timelocks a network cannot within a run,
and it prices with the Soroban SDK's 2026-07-10 snapshot of mainnet's fee
schedule. The \emph{local network} is \texttt{stellar/quickstart}, pinned by
digest, at protocol 29 with mainnet's archived Soroban settings loaded at
start (fees, cost model, entry lifetimes, limits), Stellar RPC 30.0.0,
closing about one ledger a second. \emph{Testnet} runs protocol 29 with its
own settings. \emph{Mainnet} is not run: its settings are read, and its
figures computed from them. Every approval carries a real proof of a recovery
email signed by a provider mock for the statement the real controller built.
Proving runs on an Apple M5 Max with 18 logical CPUs, 6 Super and 12
Performance cores as macOS names its two tiers, shared with other
work\claim{environments}.

\subsection{Attack suite}\label{sec:eval-attacks}
The suite deploys a registry with the proposed governance, whose launch keys
wait in their 72-hour timelock throughout the run, and enrolls each account
as the wallet does, with a 30-day pin and a member built by the wallet's own
builder. Each scenario gets the attacker's strongest input: the signature a
standard DKIM verifier accepts, the octets it covers, the claimed account's
key tree or one planted with the attacker's key, and that account's real
commitment and statement. Where a forged email still yields a witness, the
suite submits a real proof, so the refusal is the chain's, decoded to its
layer. The alert, phishing and griefing rows run the mechanisms the
email-plus-delay profile relies on, not people: the owner's cancellation is a
call the suite makes, and an alert email counts as reaching the owner only
when it goes to an address the thief does not hold. Three registry scenarios
need an elapsed timelock or grace period; they run on the test host with the
registry's production WebAssembly.

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:attack-summary} summarizes the 68 rows,
65 on the local network and 3 on the test host; every observed outcome is the
expected one\claims{network-attack-suite,registry-timelock-attacks}. Forged
emails fail at the circuit; a real proof under a key no tree holds fails
check 11; a 1024-bit key or a domain outside the allowlist fails at the
registry. A body replaced after signing counts one vote by design, since
the provider signed the header block, which is all the proof binds. Replays
fail at the first of checks 1, 2, 11 and 12 they fail, or at Perch; another
account's pin root fails check 11, because pins are salted. A planted key is
refused during its timelock, can be stopped by two members, and after
activation cannot approve without the account code. A copy submitted first
by an observer records the guardian's single vote and cannot be redirected
to a cancellation, attempt or account\claim{front-running-one-vote}.

\paragraph{The inbox thief and the alert path.} Without the account code the
thief fails at the circuit and, with a real proof for its own guess, at
check 11; the Sent-folder backup does not open without its passphrase. With
the code, under a 2-of-2 quorum the email vote leaves the attempt collecting
and is itself alerted on every channel. As the only guardian, under
\texttt{Loss} and a seven-day delay, the thief authorizes an attempt but
cannot complete it within the delay; it deletes every email sent to the
stolen inbox, which the worst allowed setup uses as the alert address, the
push to the passkey device still arrives, and the owner's cancellation
succeeds. A watcher that missed the events finds the authorized attempt from
the controller's state after it restarts\claim{compromised-inbox-alerted}.
With the push lapsed as well, the vault marks it and emails the stolen
inbox, so nothing reaches the owner; a wallet opened during the delay reads
the attempt from the controller and offers to cancel it. That row, a residual
risk, is the case A12 excludes.

\paragraph{Phishing and repeated attempts.} The victim's own email for the
phisher's statement is a valid approval: the proof binds the statement, not
intent. A victim who still holds the passkey gets a push that leads with
cancelling and cancels within the delay. A victim with no device left cannot
cancel, so under email plus delay the attempt completes, the second residual
risk (run with a delay shortened outside the wallet's policy so it elapses);
with a second helper required, the phished vote leaves it collecting. Ten
attempts opened by a stranger send the owner no email and no push; the
wallet lists all ten. No envelope, result or metadata of the run's 213
transactions contains any of its 24 inboxes' addresses, in any encoding,
account codes or pin salt leaves, a sanity check behind
Theorem~\ref{thm:privacy}, not evidence of hiding\claim{privacy-no-address-on-chain}.

An expert cognitive walkthrough of the enrollment and recovery pages, not a
user study, found sixteen issues; the wallet's safeguards of
\S\ref{sec:design-ux} answer thirteen, while iPhone notifications needing a
Home Screen install, a new phone without a mail app, and phone proving
remain open. How often people keep the code, read an alert, or resist a
phishing script is not measured\claim{user-behaviour}.

\subsection{The circuit's parsing on real and fuzzed headers}\label{sec:eval-parser}
A harness runs the circuit library with only the Subject check removed, at
the recovery circuit's bounds, and the committed recovery artifact checks its
verdicts. On 5{,}327 DKIM signatures of 5{,}154 real posts from all eleven
launch domains in eighteen public mailing-list archives, verified under the
providers' real keys, the circuit agrees with mailauth's strict verifier and
Python's address parser on every signature: 4{,}619 accepted by both with the
same address and domain, the rest refused by both. These are archived list
posts, not recovery mail, and no Fastmail signature verifies under any known
key\claim{parser-real-mail}. Over 50{,}000 seeded headers around the
\texttt{From} grammar and the tag list, every \texttt{From} the circuit
accepts is read by Python as exactly the mailbox it binds; an address list,
an address before the angle-address, a quoted word before an address and an
unclosed quote are refused. The two sides disagree only where the circuit
accepts a field the DKIM standard tells verifiers to ignore for a defect in a
tag the circuit does not read, which only the key holder can write, and
refuses one whose tag it reads has whitespace the standard
allows\claim{parser-fuzzing}.

\paragraph{Attributing the differences.} A port of the host's fee
computation reproduces every fee the networks charged from the declared
resources and archived settings. The test host prices with the SDK's
2026-07-10 snapshot; at mainnet's current settings the same transaction
costs 82{,}530 stroops before rent, under half\claim{onchain-verification-cost}. The local network runs mainnet's
settings, so its charge is the mainnet projection: 83{,}125 to 83{,}155
stroops a vote, and at most 12 votes fit a ledger\claim{network-verification-cost}.
Of the Gmail vote's 83{,}150, compute is 37{,}971 and history, the 18\,KB
envelope around the 16\,KB proof, 36{,}419; testnet charges the same
components except rent, since its temporary entries' minimum lifetime is 720
ledgers against mainnet's 17{,}280\claim{fee-attribution}. Metering differs mainly by build: testnet's
cost model moves a vote by 0.02\%, while the release-profile build meters
1.56 million fewer instructions per vote than the size-optimized wasm the
networks run\claim{metering-attribution}. Every network vote uses the pin;
a registry root adds \texttt{is\_accepted\_root}, 565{,}410 instructions, and
the registry's instance and code to the footprint\claim{registry-costs}.
Votes against a registry root on a network are not yet measured: both runs
are enrolled and vote once their additions' 72-hour attested timelock
elapses. The vote that authorizes an attempt also writes it and pays its
rent: 3{,}632{,}049 stroops, 3{,}545{,}582 of it rent\claim{authorizing-vote-cost}.

\paragraph{Archival and the keeper.} Recovery is the operation least likely
to have run recently, so the entries it reads are the likeliest to be
archived. On testnet, Perch's doc compiler and controller had been, and
restoring them cost 112{,}447{,}907 stroops, 8.4 times the 13{,}384{,}714 of
the seven-transaction recovery itself. At mainnet's settings, a recovery
with every entry it reads archived takes 7 transactions and 3{,}935{,}930{,}637
stroops, against 5{,}237{,}377 with nothing archived. The operator's keeper
extends the verifier, the registry and the guardian's code for
3{,}304{,}297{,}283 stroops a year at mainnet's settings, plus 3{,}245{,}716
per account's guardian instance\claim{testnet-recovery-total}. If it fails,
recovery still works, restoring the same bytes, and the submitter pays.

\paragraph{Phones are not measured.} No phone was available, although a
user who lost every device will often recover on a new phone. The design
budgets up to about two minutes there; that is a target, not a
result\claim{phone-proving-budget}, and whether a mobile browser holds the
1.4 to 2.5\,GiB the desktop renderer peaks at is open. The slower desktop
tier, under background scheduling, is still desktop cores, not a model of a
phone; DevTools CPU throttling leaves proving unchanged, because Chrome does
not throttle the workers in which it runs\claim{devtools-throttling}. A
self-contained benchmark page runs the same prover on any device, and the
recovery page runs in any browser, so a desktop remains a way to recover.

\subsection{Alerts, end to end, and testnet}\label{sec:eval-e2e}
With every service a loopback mock, an \texttt{attempt\_authorized} event
reaches the sending mock in 11 to 62\,ms (median 16.5) and the push mock in
13 to 66\,ms (median 19), and a restarted vault restores its key from two
custodians in 147 to 238\,ms (median 152)\claim{alert-latency}; a
deployment adds the watcher's polling interval and the providers' delivery.
On a local network, a browser test enrolls an inbox, deploys and lists its
guardian, and after the passkey is lost recovers with one email proven in
the browser and a second helper, the alert email and push carrying the code
the page shows\claim{end-to-end-local}. On testnet, one complete
email-factor recovery ran from minting to completion, after which the
account's document held the new passkey and not the lost
one\claim{testnet-email-recovery}.

\subsection{Threats to validity}\label{sec:eval-threats}
Every email is signed by a mock in its provider's published layout, and DNS,
the sending and push services, the enclave and the custodians are mocks; the
real-mail corpus checks the parser, not each provider's outbound recovery
mail. No registry addition leaves its timelock during a network run, so
network votes use the pin, and scenarios needing an active key run on the
test host, which runs the same contracts but is not a network; so does the
recovery with everything archived. The local network closes about one ledger
a second while reporting a 5-second target, so there a 120{,}960-ledger delay
lasts about 34 hours; close-time windows are unaffected. A scenario reports
only the first layer that refuses it. Timing comes from one shared machine,
testnet figures from one period, and the alert rows show mechanisms, not
people.
