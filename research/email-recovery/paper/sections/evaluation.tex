\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}

The evaluation asks whether every attack the threat model names fails at the
layer the design assigns it to (\S\ref{sec:eval-attacks}), whether the
circuit reads real and fuzzed headers as reference implementations do
(\S\ref{sec:eval-parser}), what an email vote costs in each environment and
on mainnet (\S\ref{sec:eval-cost}), and how long a user waits for a proof
(\S\ref{sec:eval-proving}); Appendix~\ref{app:repro} gives the threats to
validity.

\paragraph{Environments.} The \emph{test host} is Soroban's in-process host
in the integration tests, with Perch's controller and account from Perch's
deployed WebAssembly and every email-recovery contract from its own; its
clock can be advanced, so it reaches timelocks a network cannot within a run,
and it prices with the Soroban SDK's 2026-07-10 snapshot of mainnet's fee
schedule. The \emph{local network} is \texttt{stellar/quickstart}, pinned by
digest, at protocol 29 with mainnet's archived Soroban settings loaded at
start (fees, cost model, entry lifetimes, limits), Stellar RPC 30.0.0,
closing about one ledger a second. \emph{Testnet} runs protocol 29 with its
own settings. \emph{Mainnet} is not run: its settings are read, and its
figures computed from them. Every approval carries a real proof of a recovery
email signed by a provider mock for the statement the real controller built.
Proving runs on an Apple M5 Max with 18 logical CPUs, 6 Super and 12
Performance cores as macOS names its two tiers, shared with other
work\claim{environments}.

\subsection{Attack suite}\label{sec:eval-attacks}
The suite deploys a registry with the proposed governance, whose launch keys
wait in their 72-hour timelock throughout the run, and enrolls each account
as the wallet does, with a 30-day pin and a member built by the wallet's own
builder. Each scenario gets the attacker's strongest input: the signature a
standard DKIM verifier accepts, the octets it covers, the claimed account's
key tree or one planted with the attacker's key, and that account's real
commitment and statement. Where a forged email still yields a witness, the
suite submits a real proof, so the refusal is the chain's, decoded to its
layer. The alert, phishing and griefing rows run the mechanisms the
email-plus-delay profile relies on, not people: the owner's cancellation is a
call the suite makes, and an alert email counts as reaching the owner only
when it goes to an address the thief does not hold. Three registry scenarios
need an elapsed timelock or grace period; they run on the test host with the
registry's production WebAssembly.

\paragraph{Results.} Table~\ref{tab:attack-summary} summarizes the 73 rows,
70 on the local network and 3 on the test host; every observed outcome is the
expected one\claims{network-attack-suite,registry-timelock-attacks}. Forged
emails fail at the circuit; a real proof under a key no tree holds fails
check 11; a 1024-bit key or a domain outside the allowlist fails at the
registry. A body replaced after signing counts one vote by design: the proof
binds only the header block. Replays fail at checks 1, 2, 11 or 12, or at
Perch; another account's pin root fails check 11, because pins are salted. A
planted key is refused during its timelock and after it cannot approve
without the account code. A copy submitted first by an observer records the
guardian's single vote and cannot be redirected\claim{front-running-one-vote}.

\paragraph{The inbox thief and the alert path.} Without the account code the
thief fails at the circuit and, with a real proof for its own guess, at
check 11; the Sent-folder backup does not open without its passphrase. With
the code, under a 2-of-2 quorum the email vote leaves the attempt collecting
and is itself alerted on every channel. As the only guardian, under
\texttt{Loss} and a seven-day delay, the thief authorizes an attempt but
cannot complete it within the delay; it deletes every email sent to the
stolen inbox, which the worst allowed setup uses as the alert address, a
push is still accepted for the device the owner set up for alerts, and the
owner's cancellation succeeds. A watcher that missed the events finds the authorized attempt from
the controller's state after it restarts\claim{compromised-inbox-alerted}.
With the push lapsed as well, the vault marks it and emails the stolen
inbox, so nothing reaches the owner; a wallet opened during the delay reads
the attempt from the controller and offers to cancel it. That row, a residual
risk, is the case A12 excludes. When the owner switches phones and leaves
the old one off but subscribed, the vault marks it unacknowledged after its
missed probes, and the new phone, added at once, receives the
push\claim{wallet-second-alert-channel}.

\paragraph{Phishing and repeated attempts.} The victim's own email for the
phisher's statement is a valid approval: the proof binds the statement, not
intent. A victim who still holds the passkey gets a push that leads with
cancelling and cancels within the delay. A victim with no device left cannot
cancel, so under email plus delay the attempt completes, the second residual
risk (run with a delay shortened outside the wallet's policy so it elapses);
with a second helper required, the phished vote leaves it collecting. Ten
attempts opened by a stranger send the owner one alert, that many are open;
the wallet lists all ten. With 32 attempts opened after the voted one, the
email vote is still alerted and the wallet still lists it. No envelope,
result or metadata of the run's 263
transactions contains any of its 27 inboxes' addresses, in any encoding,
account codes or pin salt leaves, a sanity check behind
Theorem~\ref{thm:privacy}, not evidence of hiding\claim{privacy-no-address-on-chain}.

An expert walkthrough of the current pages, by their designers rather than
participants or an independent expert, leaves ten issues open, among them
that a phished user sees no warning of the operator's before sending the
email\claim{expert-walkthrough}. No user study was run: nobody enrolled,
recovered, read an alert or faced a phishing script, so how often people
keep the code, act on an alert, or resist a caller is not
measured\claim{user-behaviour}.

\subsection{The circuit's parsing on real and fuzzed headers}\label{sec:eval-parser}
A harness runs the circuit library with only the Subject check removed, at
the recovery circuit's bounds, and the committed recovery artifact checks its
verdicts. On 5{,}327 DKIM signatures of 5{,}154 real posts from all eleven
launch domains in eighteen public mailing-list archives, verified under the
providers' real keys, the circuit agrees with mailauth's strict verifier and
Python's address parser on every signature: 4{,}619 accepted by both with the
same address and domain, the rest refused by both. These are archived list
posts, not recovery mail, and no Fastmail signature verifies under any known
key\claim{parser-real-mail}. Over 50{,}000 seeded headers around the
\texttt{From} grammar and the tag list, every \texttt{From} the circuit
accepts is read by Python as exactly the mailbox it binds, and the circuit
and mailauth's strict verifier both accept 22{,}409 signatures and both
refuse 23{,}967. They disagree on 2{,}692 fields the circuit accepts though
the DKIM standard tells verifiers to ignore them for a defect in a tag the
circuit does not read, which only the key holder can write, and on 711 it
refuses for whitespace the standard allows in a tag it
reads\claim{parser-fuzzing}. Moving or replacing each unconstrained hint of
the 490 signatures the circuit accepts among the first 1{,}000 cases, 39{,}256
times in all, never binds anything else: 38{,}276 are refused and 980 bind the
same address, domain and key\claim{parser-hint-mutation}.

\paragraph{Attributing the differences.} The test host prices with the
SDK's 2026-07-10 snapshot; at mainnet's current settings the same
transaction costs 82{,}530 stroops before
rent\claim{onchain-verification-cost}. The local network runs mainnet's
settings, so its charge, 83{,}125 to 83{,}153 stroops a vote, is the mainnet
projection, and at most 12 votes fit a ledger\claim{network-verification-cost}.
A port of the host's fee computation reproduces every fee charged; of the
Gmail vote's 83{,}148, compute is 37{,}969 and history, the 18\,KB envelope
around the 16\,KB proof, 36{,}419\claim{fee-attribution}. The
release-profile build meters 1.57 million fewer instructions per vote than
the size-optimized wasm the networks run\claim{metering-attribution}. Every
network vote uses the pin;
a registry root adds \texttt{is\_accepted\_root}, 565{,}410 instructions, and
the registry's instance and code to the footprint on the test
host\claim{registry-costs}. Votes against a registry root have not been
measured on a network. The vote that authorizes an attempt also writes it
and pays its rent: 3{,}632{,}052 stroops, 3{,}545{,}582 of it
rent\claim{authorizing-vote-cost}. Enrolling costs 11{,}434{,}621 stroops
(1.1 XLM) at mainnet's settings, almost all of it rent for the new
account, its guardian and its recovery document\claim{enrollment-cost}.

\paragraph{Archival and the keeper.} Recovery is the operation least likely
to have run recently, so the entries it reads are the likeliest to be
archived. On testnet, an earlier run found Perch's doc compiler and
controller archived, and restoring them cost 112{,}447{,}907 stroops (11.2
XLM), 8.4 times the 13{,}384{,}714 of that run's seven-transaction recovery.
At mainnet's settings, a recovery with every entry it reads archived takes 7
transactions and 3{,}959{,}831{,}748 stroops (396 XLM), against 5{,}237{,}377
with nothing archived. The operator's keeper extends the verifier, the
registry and the guardian's code for 3{,}304{,}297{,}283 stroops (330.4 XLM)
a year, plus 3{,}245{,}716 (0.3246 XLM) per account's guardian instance.
These price rent at mainnet's rate when its settings were read, the
1{,}000-stroop floor per KB, which holds while the network's average state
stays at most 2.0\,GB (it was 1.718\,GB); every rent figure is 5.5 times as
large at 2.5\,GB and ten times at 3.0\,GB\claim{testnet-recovery-total}. If
the keeper fails, recovery still works, restoring the same bytes, and the
submitter pays. The recovering device holds no XLM, and the wallet has no
path for restorations too large to make inline, so who pays an archived
recovery on mainnet is open (Appendix~\ref{app:open}).

\paragraph{Phones are not measured.} No phone was measured, although a
user who lost every device will often recover on a new phone. The design
budgets up to about two minutes there, a target, not a
result\claim{phone-proving-budget}; whether a mobile browser holds the 1.4
to 2.5\,GiB the desktop renderer peaks at is open. The slower desktop tier is
not a model of a phone, and DevTools CPU throttling leaves proving unchanged,
because Chrome does not throttle workers\claim{devtools-throttling}. A
desktop remains a way to recover.

\subsection{Alerts, end to end, and testnet}\label{sec:eval-e2e}
With every service a loopback mock, an \texttt{attempt\_authorized} event
reaches the sending mock in 11 to 62\,ms (median 16.5) and the push mock in
13 to 66\,ms (median 19), and a restarted vault restores its key from two
custodians in 147 to 238\,ms (median 152)\claim{alert-latency}. On a local
network, a browser test enrolls an inbox, deploys and lists its
guardian, and after the passkey is lost recovers with one email proven in
the browser and a second helper, the alert email and push carrying the code
the page shows\claim{end-to-end-local}. On testnet, with the contracts
built from commit \texttt{ab274154} on \texttt{main} and the verifier
reporting circuit id \texttt{a998d03e}\ldots{} read back, one complete
email-factor recovery ran from minting to completion, leaving the new
passkey in the account's document; both testnet runs keep every
transaction's XDR\claim{testnet-email-recovery}.
